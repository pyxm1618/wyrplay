## Core Dilemma Index

A working semantic index was built from all 236 formal questions. Each formal item was reduced to its underlying trade-off rather than its surface nouns, age, relationship, or scenario. Candidate screening then checked eight semantic replacement risks: noun, age, scenario, relationship, fantasy skin, A/B swap, paraphrase, and same underlying trade-off.

The index was used as a negative-space map: Batch 004 preferentially explored mechanisms that were sparse in the formal bank, including sensory inference, medium/representation, information access, memory encoding, resource constraints, role utility, transformation, interpretation, evidence, atmosphere, and playful rule mechanics.

## New Dilemma Families Learned

### Sensory inference
Choices can differ by how a person detects or interprets something: sound, touch, smell, visual pattern, or another cue. This is stronger than cosmetic sensory preference when the channel changes the task.

### Representation / expression
A choice can compare two ways to represent the same idea—literal versus symbolic, visual versus verbal, polished versus playful—without collapsing into 'which medium do you like?'

### Information access
Useful dilemmas can be built around what information is available, when it becomes available, and what can be inferred, without repeating plan-ahead versus spontaneity.

### Memory encoding
Experiences can be remembered through language, objects, images, reconstruction, or shared artifacts. The trade-off is how memory is captured, not simply old versus new.

### Resource constraint
Limited pieces, tools, attempts, space, or attention can produce real trade-offs. The constraint should change strategy rather than just make one option worse.

### Role utility
Roles are stronger when they provide genuinely different functions—idea generation versus implementation, observation versus interpretation—not merely leader versus follower.

### Transformation
For younger kids especially, transforming an object, movement, drawing, or environment creates a concrete consequence that is easy to picture and explain.

### Atmosphere versus function
A place or object can optimize for usability, spaciousness, sound, coziness, spectacle, or another experiential quality. This creates more substance than cute-object A versus cute-object B.

### Interpretation versus construction
Games and classroom prompts can ask whether to decode something that exists or create something others must decode, generating different cognitive experiences.

### Evidence / perspective
Older audiences can choose between different kinds of evidence or lenses—numbers, stories, visual signals, first-hand observation—without turning the question into productivity advice.

These labels informed production but are not a front-end taxonomy. Candidate `dilemmaFamily` values remain broader so the internal system stays usable.

## Collection-specific production notes

- **Kids 4–6:** Concrete actions, visible consequences, simple powers/limits, movement, discovery, helping roles, and transformable objects. Pure cute-object pairings were rejected.
- **Kids 7–9:** Adventure, game rules, exploration, animals, creative constraints, clues, resources, and simple strategy.
- **Kids 10–12:** Fairness, strategy, skill, independence and teamwork were retained, while funny/weird/adventure material was deliberately increased.
- **Teens:** The batch shifted away from school management and scheduling toward games, hobbies, media, culture, playful social situations, imagination and harmless fantasy.
- **Adults:** The batch shifted away from productivity/home-organization routines toward leisure, culture, travel, food experiences, entertainment, creativity, odd hypotheticals and clean imaginative choices.
- **Friends:** Shared experiences, challenges, creativity, discovery and playful social hypotheticals were prioritized over who plans, who decides, or together-versus-separate.
- **Family:** Cross-age, non-comparative, low-pressure experiences and collaborative creativity were prioritized.
- **Couples:** Clean shared experiences, travel, culture, food, creative interpretation and lifestyle preferences were used while avoiding schedule/planning and shared-versus-separate repetition.
- **Coworkers:** Workplace-safe problem solving and communication were mixed with playful rituals, absurd objects and creative reframing so the set would not read like an office-process survey.
- **Classroom:** Items were written for active classroom use: movement, interpretation, explanation, rule mechanics, creative constraints and group participation.
- **Party:** Fast, low-pressure, performable choices with distinct game mechanics and social energy.
- **Road Trip:** Items depend on the drive itself: windows, passing places, passenger constraints, audio, rest stops, observation and end-of-day trip reflection.
- **Dinner:** Prompts are table-native conversation or shared-meal mechanics, not merely food preferences.
- **Date Night:** Two-person shared experiences and memory/creative mechanics, kept distinct from generic Couples questions.
- **Icebreakers:** Substantive binary choices that quickly reveal a preference or imagination; meta 'which question would you answer?' prompts were excluded.
- **Birthday Party:** Birthday-specific guest contributions, celebration artifacts and birthday-person mechanics, not generic party prompts with 'birthday' added.
- **Sleepover:** Nighttime, one-room, lights, forts, late-night creativity, quiet phases and next-morning/leave-taking rituals.
