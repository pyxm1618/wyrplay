Historical Reviewer failures were converted into generation constraints. The most important were:

1. Do not age-up an existing Kids dilemma and call it new.
2. Do not replace only activity nouns inside the same skill-choice or depth-versus-variety template.
3. Do not repackage solo-versus-social practice or ask-for-help-versus-keep-trying under a new classroom scenario.
4. A travel topic is not automatically a Road Trip scenario.
5. A relationship tag must be intrinsic to the dilemma, not merely plausible.
6. Do not bundle two unrelated variables just to make a choice look harder.
7. Avoid aesthetic-only choices with too little consequence or discussion value.
8. Fixing a safety problem does not excuse a duplicate core dilemma.
9. Planning-versus-spontaneity, music-versus-games, movie-versus-game, feedback timing, and similar axes already represented in the formal bank require special duplicate scrutiny.

## Age and collection design used in Batch 003

### Kids 4–6
Concrete objects, tiny time horizons, familiar routines, animals, toys, food and simple fantasy. A cute image was not enough; every item still had to offer two viable experiences or advantages.

### Kids 7–9
More game mechanics, creative constraints, field-trip experiences, simple consequences, first/last information advantages and light teamwork.

### Kids 10–12
More strategy, fairness, learning sequence, group decision mechanics and independence, while deliberately retaining weird, imaginative and exploratory questions so the set does not collapse into school/self-development content.

### Teens
School-life choices, media habits, hobbies, social preferences, creative work, independence and group roles. No adult relationship material, drugs/alcohol or humiliating disclosure.

### Adults
Home, routines, work-life structure, time, travel, decisions and lifestyle trade-offs. Adult does not mean explicit.

### Relationships
Friends, Family, Couples and Coworkers were only used as primary collections when the relationship changes why the question is interesting.

### Usage scenarios
Classroom, Party, Road Trip, Dinner, Date Night, Icebreakers, Birthday Party and Sleepover candidates were written for actual use in that setting, not tagged merely because the topic sounded adjacent.

## Cross-history duplicate prevention

The 116 formal questions were treated as immutable semantic anchors. Historical REJECT and duplicate reasons from Foundation Batch 002 were also used as negative examples.

The initial 146-question draft went through:

1. normalized exact-question comparison;
2. unordered A/B comparison, including swapped options;
3. lexical token-set similarity against every formal question;
4. pairwise internal similarity screening;
5. manual semantic review of high-similarity results;
6. manual review for historically rejected axes even when lexical overlap was low;
7. template-saturation review across collections.

### Ideas actively removed before finalizing

- **14 draft ideas** were replaced because their core dilemma was too close to the formal 116-question bank or to a historically rejected duplicate axis. Examples of rejected draft directions included first-vs-last-after-observing, early-vs-late feedback, compressed-vs-distributed time, old-vs-new tradition, couple plan-turn-taking, solo-vs-social learning input, road-trip planning-vs-spontaneity, local-food repetition, old-vs-new ritual, shared-hobby-vs-new-skill icebreaker, no-phone-vs-limited-screen time, and one-big-game-vs-several-games.
- **2 internal draft ideas** were replaced because another Batch 003 item already expressed the same core mechanism: early-vs-late party-theme disclosure across Party/Birthday Party, and choose-vs-random activity selection across Dinner/Sleepover.
- After replacement, final exact duplicates, swapped A/B duplicates and internal exact duplicates are all **0**.
